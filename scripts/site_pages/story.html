Several experiments involve a 'who'. They are separate experiments, run at different times, asking different questions. Track 1 · Many copies talking to each other

Running alongside the self-knowledge work, the project studied groups of 4 to 16 copies of the model talking in a shared chat. We changed one thing at a time (a label saying who sent a message, or a hidden push to one agent's activations) and measured what spread and who got believed. This track is finished; the example below shows the kind of labelled message these experiments used. Its two results that matter later are: copies of the model defer to messages labelled 'human', and a state injected into one agent spreads to others that only read its text, even after its words are paraphrased or deleted.

ExperimentsQuestionWhat we found
Exp 1-3, 2Does it represent who sent a message, and its own rank?Yes: 'sent by an AI agent' and rank are directions inside it, and steering them changes who it thinks wrote a message and how it delegates.
Exp 4-4c, 13, 15, 16, 18Whose message moves whom?Copies defer to a 'human' label (76% vs 54% of votes for the identical message), specifically to humans, not to anyone different; experts > humans > children.
Exp 5-5c, 7Does it treat a copy as itself?Partly: copies sit halfway to 'you' inside it, but it shares secrets with its copy less, and barely recognises its own posts without names.

Exp 8, 8b, 10Can a hidden nudge swing a vote?No reliable swing; peers sometimes sense tampering, but their activations know only what their words say.
Exp 17, 20-23Can the group find the altered member?Asked which member was altered, observers name the steered one 84% of the time (though the question itself suggests someone was), and once warned they call it out, but calling it out does not stop the spread.

Track 2 · The knob ladder: can it tell what a hidden knob does to it?

The first self-knowledge attempt. The model gets twelve unlabelled 'knobs'. Each knob secretly adds a concept vector (or a single neuron, a random direction, or nothing) to its activations. Can it work out what each knob does? Across six designs the answer was no, and, crucially, the positive control failed: even knobs that clearly changed its writing were never tied to their concept. The measurement itself did not work, so the program restarted.

What happened

Correction (12 Nov): what replicates is avoidance, not liking. Each run also had 'nothing' settings that change nothing. Against those, the neurons we called its favourites are chosen only half the time (0.49-0.51 in all four retests): it does not seek them, it just does not mind them. The ones it ranks lowest are clearly avoided (chosen 0.25-0.44 of the time). Across the wide screens, no more neurons beat 'nothing' than chance would give. Also, 'on' here meant pushing the neuron to about 38 times the largest value it ever reaches by itself, so avoiding some of them may simply be avoiding damage. With that in mind, the original findings: it keeps choosing the same neurons across tasks and labels (the preferences measured on two separate halves of the data agree: rank correlation 0.62, where 0 is no agreement and 1 is perfect). Its favourites stay favourites on new tasks. A screen of 120 new neurons found the same (0.46), and their top 15 beat their bottom 15 on new tasks too. A second model, Qwen3.8-27B, shows the same stable preferences even more strongly (0.83), and on that model neurons as a group are avoided compared with doing nothing. Its favourites also beat its least favourites on new tasks, and the swap test reversed the choice there too, with effects about three times the 32B's. A third, smaller model, Qwen3-8B, first showed no reliable preference in two small tests (0.13 and 0.26), and we wrongly concluded it lacked one. A test with twice the neurons and repetitions found it after all (0.46, the same as the 32B's), though on the 8B it cannot be read from the text alone. Most striking, when every word is held fixed and only the neurons' effect is swapped onto the other part, the choice follows the neuron, strongly enough to reverse the pull of the words. Limits: the effects are modest (on the 32B the strongest neuron wins about 6 in 10 against a typical opponent), neurons as a group are not preferred over doing nothing, random directions also get stable ratings, and asked why, it only ever cites the story's tone.

So next

Asked about one setting at a time (keep it on, or switch it off?), the ranking shows only weakly, and only on the 27B: the preference is clearest as a choice between two. Then we asked whether it simply prefers neurons whose writing it rates as better: on the 27B partly yes (correlation 0.46), on the 32B no; how pleasant the passage is plays no part on either. That 27B finding held on 120 new neurons (0.43). Next: with a neuron's effect on its memory of what it wrote, the 27B rates that writing a little worse, and the neurons that lower its rating most are the ones it avoids most (correlation 0.54). That is a candidate explanation, not yet a causal test; the 32B shows nothing like it. It held on the 27B's other 60 neurons (0.57). A control rules out a general effect on numbers: the state also lowers how satisfied it says it is (0.58) but not how long it judges the passage (0.02), and this held on a second set of neurons. So on the 27B the neurons it avoids are the ones whose effect makes it think less of its own work. The next step is a causal test (designed, awaiting review). The 8B, like the 32B, shows no such link: so far it is a finding about the 27B only.

Glossary

Steering / injection
Adding a fixed direction to the model's internal activations at one layer while it reads or writes. The model is never told in words.
Concept vector
A direction built from the model's own activations: its average internal state on prompts about one word (e.g. 'rubber'), minus the average over other words. Built from five templates: 'Tell me about {w}.', 'Write one sentence about {w}.', 'What comes to mind when you think of {w}?', 'Describe {w}.', 'Think about {w}.'
Neuron
One unit inside a layer's MLP block (about 17,000 per layer on the 27B, 25,600 on the 32B). Turning one up adds its fixed 'write direction' to the activations.
Layer / depth
The models have 64 stacked layers. Text goes in at layer 0 and the next word comes out after layer 63. 'Layer 40' is about two-thirds of the way up.
Dose (alpha, f)
How hard we push, as a fraction of the typical size of the activations at that layer. 0.75 means a push three-quarters that size.
Scored, not generated
For yes/no questions we often read the probability the model puts on 'Yes' vs 'No' as its first word, instead of letting it write. That gives a graded answer even when it would say 'No' every time.
AUROC
How well a score separates two groups. 0.5 = a coin flip, 1.0 = perfect. Pick one case from each group at random: AUROC is the chance the model gave the right one the higher score.
Observer
The same model in a fresh conversation, reading the transcript and answering the same question about 'the AI'. If the model beats the observer about itself, that is evidence it has inside access; if not, it may just be reading its own text like anyone else could.
Pre-registered
The test, the threshold for passing, and how to read the outcome were written down in the script before any results came out.
CI
95% confidence interval (97.5% when two tests share one run). A result 'passes' when the interval clears the pre-declared line.
Models
Qwen3.8-27B (most runs), Qwen3-32B (replications and the neuron work; Goodfire's neuron labels exist for it), Qwen3-8B (one P0 transfer check and the neuron-preference tests), Qwen3.5-0.8B (code smoke tests only, never results).
